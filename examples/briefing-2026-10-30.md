## The guest

Jo is a student letterpress maker and a thoughtful reader of Dickinson's manuscripts, working in a visual and material register. Her piece, *Alabaster*, is three letterpress broadsides (two versions of "Safe in their Alabaster Chambers" and Susan Dickinson's objection) plus a 90-second top-down stop-motion of the second stanza being reset in metal. The repository marks it finished and printed; the one known fix is sheet three's heavy impression, which she intends to reprint.

## The one thing

The piece tries to make a variant cost something: a word change in metal means pulling sorts, respacing lines, and shifting the whole form, so "choosing not choosing" (Cameron's phrase) becomes a physical choice Jo has to actually make.

## Beats

**Origin in a sophomore letterpress class.** Ask about the slowness of changing one word and the moment she connected it to the editor's note on Dickinson's unchosen variants; her dictated line "what if you had to pick. Physically" is the anchor.

**Why this poem.** The notes say the variant is a whole stanza, not a word, and that Sue's objection is a real 1861 quarrel between neighbors. Ask why the stanza-scale fight mattered more than a one-word change, and what she wanted the viewer to feel about it.

**The film's premise and its silence.** Her description and notes say "no voiceover" and room tone only, but the notes give only "I think" as the reason and her Oct 28 post says she could not yet state it in one sentence. Give her the space to try, on air.

**The counting and the structure.** The 212 sorts out and 247 in, and the replacement-not-revision insight, came from a script Claude wrote and she edited. Ask her to walk through what the count revealed that she had not expected.

**The edit and what she rejected.** The edit log records a Claude suggestion to cut to the rhythm of the clicks, which she tried, judged "too neat," and dropped. Ask what made her trust that judgment, and what she kept (the accidental stick drop).

**Printing and the question of what counts.** Twelve copies, the press schedule, and whether that is an edition or a proof run. The repository leaves this open; ask whether Madeleine resolved it, and whether it changes how she describes the work.

## Questions

1. In your own words, what did you want a viewer to understand about choosing that they couldn't get from just reading the two versions side by side?
2. Which decisions in this piece were entirely yours, and which did a tool shape? Take the script, the cover attempts, and the edit as separate cases.
3. Claude wrote the word-diff script and you changed the tokenizer to keep dashes. What did the tool give you, and what did you have to know already to fix it?
4. You rejected three AI-generated cover images and a rhythm-based cut. What about those outputs made them wrong for this piece, and what does that tell you about where you stand as the maker?
5. What didn't work? Tell me about a typesetting study, a framing test, or the heavy impression on sheet three that you had to abandon or redo.
6. The film has no voiceover. If you had to explain that choice to someone who thought it was an absence, what would you say?

## Names and terms

- **Emily Dickinson** (ih-MILLY DICK-in-son): the poet; the source of both versions.
- **"Safe in their Alabaster Chambers"**: the poem, in 1859 and 1861 versions with variant second stanzas.
- **Susan Gilbert Dickinson**: Emily's sister-in-law and confidante, who objected to the 1861 second stanza.
- **Fascicles**: the hand-stitched bundles in which Dickinson kept many poems.
- **Sharon Cameron**, *Choosing Not Choosing* (1992): argues variants are kept open on purpose; the idea the piece argues with.
- **Susan Howe**, *My Emily Dickinson* (1985): critiques editors for regularizing the manuscripts.
- **Cristanne Miller**, ed., *Emily Dickinson's Poems: As She Preserved Them* (2016): the comparison edition Jo used.
- **Marta Werner and Jen Bervin**, *The Gorgeous Nothings* (2013): facsimiles of the envelope poems; source of the idea that paper shape is part of the writing.
- **Caslon**: an 1720s typeface family, set at 14 pt.
- **Vandercook SP-15**: a flatbed proof press.
- **Sort**: one piece of metal type; the film's counts refer to sorts.
- **Chase**: the metal frame that holds set type on the press.
- **Broadside**: a single printed sheet.
