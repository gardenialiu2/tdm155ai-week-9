# Producers and game masters: models for a layer over a cast of agents

Reality television is made by two kinds of producing: producers in the field who set up situations and interview the cast, and story producers in post-production who find a narrative in hundreds of hours of footage and hand editors a plan for each scene. Its two most useful devices for a theater of agents are the confessional, a private interview to camera that the edit uses to tell the audience what a character thinks, and the format, a written set of rules (challenges, votes, eliminations, twists) that turns a cast into a game. Game masters and Mafia moderators are the live counterpart: one person who holds information the players don't, runs the phases, and announces outcomes, sometimes bending the dice. Together they suggest a producer layer with three jobs (casting, injecting events, and editing the record) and two habits worth refusing even with agents, which are putting words in a character's mouth and hiding the edit from the audience. The writers' room and improv are covered in *Production crews and ensembles* (essay 04 of the Lab's *Orchestration across industries* series, in the `mk-agent-studies` repository), sections 6 and 7; this note does not repeat them.

## Reality television: who does what

The trade descriptions agree on a split between people who shoot and people who shape. Executive producers oversee the whole show; story producers work episode by episode and are "often the interface between the edit, the executives overseeing post, and the showrunners" (verified: read [Wrapbook, Weisenberger 2023](https://wrapbook.com/blog/what-does-a-story-producer-do-on-reality-tv)). A veteran producer quoted there calls the story producer "essentially the scriptwriter [in unscripted TV]" (verified: same source).

Field producers work with the cast during the shoot. An anonymous Australian producer with more than a decade in the business told VICE that "part of my job when I meet them is to basically become their best friend," and that "the story power all goes to the executives and the networks" (verified: read [VICE, Fenwick 2022](https://vice.com/en/article/theres-100-manipulation-the-inner-workings-of-reality-tv-according-to-a-producer)). Story producers' duties, per a working reality editor's blog, can include "field logging, writing hot sheets, cutting stringouts, and creating frankenbites," and "the episode basically only exists in the story producer's head until there is a rough cut" (verified: read [Reality 101 blog, "What is a Story Producer?", 2011](https://frankenbiter.blogspot.com/2011)). Andy Dehnart summarizes the job as assembling "a coherent narrative from the messiness of real life and a reality TV production," and quotes the producer Jay Blumenfield: "someone hands me a giant bag of broken glass, and I make a mosaic" (verified: read [reality blurred, Dehnart 2015](https://www.realityblurred.com/realitytv/2015/09/reality-tv-story-producer-job-explained/)).

Casting producers and segment producers appear in job listings, but I found no article describing them that I could read; they are named here from general knowledge (unverified: no source read).

## Finding the story after the fact

The story is found in post-production more than it is planned. Troy DeVolld, a former story producer on *The Bachelor* and *Dancing with the Stars*, says "everything has to start with a plan" and that there is "some sort of outline written before you go to the field," but that it changes once the cast takes the crew somewhere; across roughly four months of footage, the job is finding "six or eight scenes that relate to each other" (verified: read [The A.V. Club, Herzog 2013](https://www.avclub.com/how-reality-tv-gets-written-1798239001)). Story producers review all the footage for an episode, outline the story on paper, then build assemblies of the key beats in the edit and hand them to an editor (verified: read Wrapbook).

The handoff document is the **string-out**: "a sequence of shots that a Story Producer assembles and gives to an Editor," a "road map" for the scene, not an edit; the guide says not to add music, and puts a reasonable string-out at 15 to 25 minutes for one to two hours of raw material (verified: read Steven Friedland, "The Perfect Stringout," reposted on [Reality 101](https://frankenbiter.blogspot.com/2011)). On union shows, the same blog says, story producers sometimes write string-outs as lists of tape numbers and timecodes that an assistant editor assembles, a practice called papercutting (verified: read Reality 101). Interview **bites** are tagged by topic so they can be found later; one tip is to slate each interview topic with a numbered sheet (verified: read Reality 101).

The edit can contradict what happened. In a story Dehnart relays, the producer Jon Collins says of a *Project Runway* storyline that "most of the freak-out happens off camera," and that he "strung it out as if they're laughing and having fun" because "we didn't really have the footage in order to back up what she was saying"; Tony Marsh's comment was that "you've sort of made something that didn't exist, but it sort of did" (verified: read reality blurred 2015). The edit also decides what exists at all: a live Tribal Council in the first episode of *Survivor: Winners at War* "was completely edited out of the broadcast" (verified: read [The Ringer, Reinstein 2020](https://www.theringer.com/tv/2020/5/12/21254206/survivor-tribal-council-evolution-behind-the-scenes)).

## Confessionals

The confessional is the private channel. On *The Real World* (MTV, 1992), each week housemates sat alone and spoke to the camera about recent events; producers asked for complete sentences so viewers felt addressed, and Judd Winick called it "like therapy without the help" (verified: read [Wikipedia, "The Real World"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Real_World_(TV_series))). Wikipedia credits the Dutch *Nummer 28* (1991) with "the interspersing of events on screen with after-the-fact" confessionals (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Reality television"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reality_television)). *Big Brother* has a fixed room for it: in the Diary Room "contestants can privately express their feelings about the game," and the producer, speaking as "Big Brother," "issues directives and commands to contestants" (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Big Brother (franchise)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Brother_(franchise))). In *Survivor*, every eliminated player gives a final confessional (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Survivor (franchise)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivor_(franchise))).

How they are prompted matters for agents, because it is prompt design. A reality editor's guide says to frame questions as sentences to finish ("When I first see Johnny, I'm thinking..."), to have subjects speak in the present tense "like you're telling a joke," to use names instead of pronouns, and "never, ever ask yes or no questions" (verified: read Reality 101, "How to Conduct a Reality TV Interview PART 1," 2011). DeVolld opens hard questions with "If this is true..." so subjects don't shut down (verified: read A.V. Club). The VICE producer describes a "master interview" of "an hour, two hours, sometimes more" (verified: read VICE). A story producer told Bustle that interviews are planned after reviewing the episode's footage, and that a topic a cast member avoids on camera "might come back to bite you during confessional interviews" (verified: read [Bustle, Framke 2026](https://www.bustle.com/entertainment/reality-tv-producers-behind-the-scenes)). The industry acronyms ITM ("in the moment") and OTF ("on the fly," an impromptu interview in the field) are both in use; I verified OTF only (verified: read [Nevada Film Office search result](https://nevadafilm.com/?p=9593), snippet only; ITM defined in Refinery29 as an "In The Moment" interview, verified: read [Refinery29, Morin 2021](https://www.refinery29.com/en-us/2021/08/10627696/the-bachelorette-producers-editing-frankenbiting-manipulation)).

## Frankenbites

A **frankenbite** is an interview line assembled from pieces recorded at different times. The term dates at least to 2004, when the *Washington Post* reported that reality writers "use the expression 'frankenbites' (after Dr. Frankenstein's monster)" (verified: read the citation as quoted by [A Way with Words](https://www.waywordradio.org/frankenbite_3/); the Post article itself unread). Practitioners admit it and draw the line in different places. DeVolld defines it as cutting "original spoken material" into a shorter or different version, mainly for "compression of time," with the test being whether the line stays "authentic to their character"; he admits changing a location in a line to set up a scene, calling it "mixing the spackle to hold the show together" (verified: read A.V. Club). A producer quoted by Wrapbook is blunter: "You're taking a lot of interviews and putting pieces together to make the talent say what you want them to say" (verified: read Wrapbook). The VICE producer calls them "franken-grabs," says fabricating whole conversations is rare, and says "it's a lot easier to give someone the hero cut, it's harder to give someone the villain cut" (verified: read VICE). Viewers of *The Bachelorette* in 2021 caught suspected splices by pitch changes and mismatched faces (verified: read Refinery29). Manfred Becker's dissertation and Routledge book survey editors on these dilemmas (verified: read [YorkSpace abstract](https://yorkspace.library.yorku.ca/xmlui/handle/10315/38136); book itself unread).

## Casting for conflict

Casting is persona design done in advance. *The Real World* chose strangers from thousands of applicants for a mix of "different races, sexes, sexual orientations, levels of sexual experience, and religious and political beliefs" (verified: read Wikipedia, "The Real World"). The VICE producer says shows keep some people "for a while because they're going to be a villain," and that contestants "have no idea who they are going to be" (verified: read VICE). Producers told Bustle they "prefer an overly dramatic cast member to one who's too guarded," and a story producer complained that guarded people are "already producing themselves" (verified: read Bustle). Jennifer Pozner, author of *Reality Bites Back* (2010), argues that early *Real World* casts weren't "specifically casting for bigots" but later shows cast "people with anger-management problems, and people with emotional instability" (verified: read [San Antonio Current interview, Harman 2011](https://www.sacurrent.com/?p=325820); book itself unread).

## Formats, rules, and twists

A competition format is a written game. *Survivor*'s, developed by Charlie Parsons for Planet 24 in 1992 and first aired as Sweden's *Expedition Robinson* in 1997, splits castaways into tribes; challenges win reward or immunity; the losing tribe goes to Tribal Council and votes someone out; after the merge, the voted-out form a jury that chooses the winner (verified: read Wikipedia, "Survivor (franchise)"). Twists alter the rules mid-game: hidden immunity idols found through clues, Exile Island, Redemption Island (verified: same). Mark Burnett wanted Tribal Council "at night and an intimidating/spooky experience," with the vote as the moment viewers "see the uncertainty on the faces"; the host Jeff Probst refused an earpiece so he could "put myself inside the story," preparing "topics I want to explore" from producers' bullet points rather than watching the footage (verified: read The Ringer). *Big Brother* (Netherlands, 1999, created by John de Mol Jr.) runs on private nominations, weekly evictions, public phone votes, tasks, and twists such as secret missions and fake evictions (verified: read Wikipedia, "Big Brother (franchise)").

The rules travel in a **format bible**, a production manual that lets a licensee "reproduce your show in any market without the creator's direct involvement," covering mechanics (a "beat-by-beat breakdown" of an episode, scoring, elimination, winner determination), casting criteria, and which elements can and can't be changed (verified: read [Vitrina, 2026](https://vitrina.ai/blog/tv-format-bible-commissioned-2026/), a trade marketing blog; real bibles are confidential).

## Precedents

*An American Family* (PBS, 1973) followed the Loud family of Santa Barbara, edited from about 300 hours of film shot from May 1971; during filming the parents divorced. Pat Loud first praised it, then said it "makes us look like a bunch of freaks and monsters"; producer Craig Gilbert said "I didn't script a thing" (verified: read [Wikipedia, "An American Family"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_American_Family)). *The Real World*'s creators, Mary-Ellis Bunim and Jonathan Murray, cite it as their inspiration, and considered a scripted show first but dropped it because writers and actors cost too much (verified: read Wikipedia, "The Real World"). That economic fact matters for the labor debate. Mark Andrejevic's *Reality TV: The Work of Being Watched* (2004) reads the genre as "propaganda for a new business model" in which being monitored is the work; the *New York Times* noted cast members "can wind up making little more than minimum wage" for the months they spend on camera (verified: read [NYT, Eakin 2004, reposted](https://16beavergroup.org/articles/2004/01/17/greeting-big-brother-with-open-arms); book itself unread).

## Producers as uncredited writers

The Writers Guild of America, West spent years arguing that story producers are writers. In July 2005 it sued four networks (ABC, CBS, The WB, TBS) and four production companies on behalf of twelve reality "writers," Troy DeVolld among them, over unpaid overtime and falsified time cards (verified: read [reality blurred, Dehnart 2005](https://www.realityblurred.com/realitytv/2005/07/industry-news-writers_sue/)). In July 2006 story writers on *America's Next Top Model* cycle 7 struck over the producers' refusal of WGA representation, saying "our peers in dramatic television work under the protections of a WGA contract" (verified: read [reality blurred, Dehnart 2006](https://www.realityblurred.com/realitytv/2006/07/top-model-7-writers_strike/)). During the 2007 strike, picketers carried signs reading "Unscripted? Yeah, write," and a former *Top Model* story producer said reality shows are "half-written before the shows are shot" (verified: read [reality blurred, Dehnart 2007](https://www.realityblurred.com/realitytv/2007/12/industry-news-strike_reality_tv_issues/)). In 2013 DeVolld said "reality television is not covered by the WGA" (verified: read A.V. Club). Reported settlements of the overtime suits (about $2.57 million and $1.54 million) came from search snippets of *The Hollywood Reporter* I did not open (unverified: snippet only).

## Game masters and moderators

The tabletop **game master** is the oldest written-down version of a producer with private knowledge. *Dungeons & Dragons* (Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson, TSR, 1974) has a Dungeon Master who "serves as referee and storyteller," plays the non-player characters, decides results by the rules and their own reading of them, and "may choose to deviate from the published rules"; players keep character sheets, and premade adventures are called modules (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Dungeons & Dragons"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungeons_%26_Dragons)). Other systems call the role judge, narrator, referee, director, storyteller, or Keeper (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Gamemaster"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamemaster)).

The **gamemaster's screen** is the hidden-information boundary made physical: it keeps "the gamemaster's private information and dice rolls" from the players. The first commercial one was Judges Guild's Judge's Shield (1977); TSR's Dungeon Masters Screen (1979) put art on the players' side (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Gamemaster's screen"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamemaster%27s_screen)). Behind it, a GM can **fudge**: roll one thing and call out another, usually "for the excitement of the game" or "to keep the players from dying"; critics say it hollows out the dice, and one group's advice is to fudge sparingly and "never let your players know" (verified: read [Nerdarchy, 2019](https://nerdarchy.com/dd-dice-fudging-is-it-cheating/)). A line often attributed to Gygax's 1979 *Dungeon Masters Guide*, "It is your right to control the dice at any time," circulates in forums; I couldn't check it against the book (unverified: secondhand forum quote).

The **Mafia moderator** is a GM stripped to the protocol. Dmitry Davidoff, a psychology student at Moscow State University, created Mafia around 1986 (Wikipedia gives both 1986 and spring 1987 for the first game); Andrew Plotkin rethemed it as Werewolf in 1997 (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Mafia (party game)"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafia_(party_game))). The moderator doesn't play, assigns roles secretly by card or tap, knows every role, runs alternating phases (at night everyone closes their eyes and the mafiosi silently pick a victim; by day the moderator announces who died and the town debates and votes someone out by majority), privately signals investigative results to a detective or seer, and in some variants may show false results (verified: same).

The **referee** and **host** sit at the light end. ComedySportz uses "a referee who awards points and administers fouls"; Keith Johnstone's Theatresports (Calgary, 1977) took its devices from professional wrestling and is rated by judges or the audience (verified: read [Wikipedia, "Theatresports"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theatresports)). Jeff Probst at Tribal Council is a host who is also, in Parvati Shallow's words, "a key player" (verified: read The Ringer).

## Roles at a glance

| Role | What they control | What they write down | Agent-system analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive producer / showrunner | The show's direction; final calls with the network | Notes, approvals | The orchestrator's config and the human who signs off |
| Casting producer | Who is in the cast and what types they fill | Casting criteria, profiles | Persona design: the system prompts for each character |
| Field producer | Rapport; situations; on-the-fly interviews | Field logs, hot sheets | An agent that prompts characters during a run and tags moments |
| Story producer | What the episode is about; which bites go where | Outline, string-out, frankenbites | A post-run agent that reads the full log and writes a narrative |
| Editor | The final cut | The cut itself | The renderer that turns a narrative into the viewed record |
| Confessional interviewer | The private channel; the questions asked | Interview bites, slated by topic | A "to camera" prompt each character answers privately |
| Format owner | Rules, phases, scoring, twists | The format bible | A rules file the producer agent reads and enforces |
| Game master | The world, NPCs, rulings, pacing | Module, notes behind the screen | A world model with hidden state and an adjudicator |
| Mafia moderator | Phases, secret roles, announcements | The role list | A deterministic referee holding private state and a public log |
| Referee / host | Points, fouls, the ceremony | Score | A scorer that writes to the dashboard |

## What it lends to a theater of agents

**Two channels per character, kept apart.** Public speech goes to the shared transcript; confessionals go to a private channel only producers and observers read. The confessional prompt can borrow the editors' rules: ask it to finish a sentence, in the present tense, naming the other characters, never yes/no. "If this is true..." is a usable opener for a character who refuses to engage.

**Casting is the system prompt.** Producers cast for types and conflict before anything happens; students can write cast sheets the same way, including whose objectives collide, and can vary casting as the experiment's independent variable.

**The format is a file, the twist is an event.** Write the rules (phases, challenges, votes, elimination) as a format bible the producer agent reads. A twist is an injected event with a time and a trigger, logged as such: "Exile Island at round 4" is a cue, like the stage manager's in the prompt book.

**The moderator holds state the characters don't.** Mafia shows how far a referee can be deterministic: secret role assignment, night actions collected privately, morning announcements, a majority vote. Characters see only their own role and the public log; the moderator sees everything.

**The edit is the observer's job, and it should show its seams.** Story producers find story after the fact, from logs, into string-outs. In the third system, observers can do the same: read the public log and the confessionals, pick six or eight related moments, and write the episode. Keep the string-out (the list of cited turns) next to the narrative, so the dashboard can show what each claim rests on.

**Hidden information is a design choice with a cost.** A screen lets the GM fudge; every hidden roll is a place the record can diverge from what happened. Logging every producer intervention, including fudges, keeps the experiment readable.

## Where the analogy breaks

**Agents aren't harmed, but the habits are ours.** Reality TV's documented abuses (the Louds' "freaks and monsters," producers turning people into villains, editors saying things "didn't exist, but it sort of did") are harms to people. A character agent can't be humiliated. What students practice, though, is the producer's reflex: deciding the villain first and editing toward it. That habit transfers to journalism, documentary, and data storytelling, where the subjects are real.

**Frankenbiting an agent is easy and invisible.** With a transcript, a producer agent can splice lines into a confession the character never made, at zero cost and with no pitch change to give it away. The rule for the class should be the strict one: observers quote turns whole, with IDs, and any condensation is marked.

**Consent doesn't apply, which is the lesson.** Reality casts sign releases; agents sign nothing. The class can name what consent would mean if a character were based on a real person, a classmate, or a public figure, and keep characters fictional.

**Agents produce themselves.** Producers complain about guarded people who are "already producing themselves." Language models do this by default: they know they are in a story and perform for it. Confessionals from a model may be more performance than private thought.

**A deterministic moderator isn't a GM.** The best GMs and hosts improvise with the table; Probst refused the earpiece to stay inside the story. A rules engine runs phases fairly but won't sense when a scene is dying. That judgment is what a producer agent, or a student, adds.

## Glossary candidates

### Story producer
**In AI engineering.** An agent that reads a run's full log after the fact and writes the narrative: what happened, to whom, and why it matters. It selects; it shouldn't invent.
**In human practice.** The reality-TV producer who finds an episode's story in the footage, outlines it, and hands editors string-outs; often called the scriptwriter of unscripted TV.
**In the theater of agents.** After 40 rounds, the story-producer agent picks the six turns that make the alliance's collapse an episode.
**Sources.** [Wrapbook](https://wrapbook.com/blog/what-does-a-story-producer-do-on-reality-tv) (verified); [reality blurred](https://www.realityblurred.com/realitytv/2015/09/reality-tv-story-producer-job-explained/) (verified)

### Confessional
**In AI engineering.** A private output channel: the agent is prompted to say, to no other character, what it thinks and intends. Useful for eliciting reasoning that public turns hide.
**In human practice.** A cast member's interview alone to camera, cut into scenes to explain motives; *The Real World*'s weekly sessions and *Big Brother*'s Diary Room are the models.
**In the theater of agents.** After each vote, every character answers "Finish this sentence: right now I'm thinking..." into a channel only observers see.
**Sources.** [Wikipedia, The Real World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Real_World_(TV_series)) (verified); [Reality 101](https://frankenbiter.blogspot.com/2011) (verified)

### Frankenbite
**In AI engineering.** A quotation assembled from fragments of different turns, presented as one utterance. In an agent log it is trivial to make and impossible to detect without turn IDs.
**In human practice.** An interview line spliced from pieces recorded at different times; defended as time compression, criticized as putting words in mouths.
**In the theater of agents.** The class rule: observers may quote only whole turns with IDs, and the dashboard flags any spliced quote.
**Sources.** [The A.V. Club](https://www.avclub.com/how-reality-tv-gets-written-1798239001) (verified); [A Way with Words](https://www.waywordradio.org/frankenbite_3/) (verified)

### String-out
**In AI engineering.** An ordered list of log references (turn IDs, timestamps) handed from a selecting agent to a writing or rendering agent; the evidence trail beneath a summary.
**In human practice.** A sequence of shots a story producer assembles for an editor as a road map, not a finished edit.
**In the theater of agents.** The observer writes `string-out.json` (turns 12, 15, 31, confessional 4) before drafting the episode recap.
**Sources.** [Reality 101, "The Perfect Stringout"](https://frankenbiter.blogspot.com/2011) (verified)

### Casting
**In AI engineering.** Persona design: choosing which characters exist and writing their system prompts so that their goals and temperaments produce interaction worth reading.
**In human practice.** Choosing a reality cast for type and conflict, from thousands of applicants, sometimes with villains in mind.
**In the theater of agents.** Rerun the same format with one character recast from cautious to reckless and compare the transcripts.
**Sources.** [VICE](https://vice.com/en/article/theres-100-manipulation-the-inner-workings-of-reality-tv-according-to-a-producer) (verified); [Wikipedia, The Real World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Real_World_(TV_series)) (verified)

### Format bible
**In AI engineering.** The rules file the producer and moderator agents read: phases, challenges, scoring, elimination, twists, and which parts may vary.
**In human practice.** The production manual licensed with a TV format so another company can make a local version without the creator.
**In the theater of agents.** `format.md` defines three rounds of challenge, vote, and elimination, and lists the twists the producers may inject.
**Sources.** [Vitrina](https://vitrina.ai/blog/tv-format-bible-commissioned-2026/) (verified; marketing source)

### Twist
**In AI engineering.** An injected event: the orchestrator changes the environment or rules at a set time or trigger, and the change is logged as an intervention.
**In human practice.** A mid-game rule change in competition reality TV, such as hidden immunity idols, Exile Island, or fake evictions.
**In the theater of agents.** At round 4 the producers announce that the next vote is secret and the loser picks who goes home.
**Sources.** [Wikipedia, Survivor (franchise)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivor_(franchise)) (verified); [Wikipedia, Big Brother (franchise)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Brother_(franchise)) (verified)

### Elimination
**In AI engineering.** Removing an agent from the active set by a rule (a vote, a score), which changes every remaining agent's context.
**In human practice.** Voting a player out at Tribal Council or evicting a housemate by public vote.
**In the theater of agents.** The moderator tallies the characters' votes and removes the loser, who gives one final confessional.
**Sources.** [Wikipedia, Survivor (franchise)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Survivor_(franchise)) (verified)

### Game master
**In AI engineering.** An adjudicating agent that holds the world state, plays the non-player parts, and rules on what each character's action does.
**In human practice.** The referee and storyteller of a tabletop role-playing game, the Dungeon Master in D&D (1974).
**In the theater of agents.** A character says it searches the vault; the GM agent decides what it finds and tells only that character.
**Sources.** [Wikipedia, Dungeons & Dragons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungeons_%26_Dragons) (verified); [Wikipedia, Gamemaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamemaster) (verified)

### Moderator
**In AI engineering.** A deterministic or mostly deterministic referee that assigns secret roles, runs phases, collects private actions, and posts public announcements.
**In human practice.** The non-playing narrator of Mafia or Werewolf, who knows every role and runs the night and day phases.
**In the theater of agents.** The moderator script whispers each character its secret role, then announces each morning who left the house.
**Sources.** [Wikipedia, Mafia (party game)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mafia_(party_game)) (verified)

### Hidden information
**In AI engineering.** State some agents can see and others can't; enforced by giving each agent only its own projection of the state.
**In human practice.** What sits behind the gamemaster's screen: notes, maps, and dice rolls the players shouldn't see.
**In the theater of agents.** Only the producers and observers see the confessionals; characters see only the public channel.
**Sources.** [Wikipedia, Gamemaster's screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamemaster%27s_screen) (verified)

### Fudging
**In AI engineering.** An orchestrator silently overriding a random or rule-based outcome to steer the story; it should at least be logged.
**In human practice.** A GM rolling one result behind the screen and announcing another, usually to save a character or the evening.
**In the theater of agents.** The producers may overrule one vote per season, and the dashboard marks it as an intervention.
**Sources.** [Nerdarchy](https://nerdarchy.com/dd-dice-fudging-is-it-cheating/) (verified)
